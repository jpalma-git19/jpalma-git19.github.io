The Great Corporate Con: How the World's Biggest Brands Profit from Destruction While Selling Us a Smile

Smiling Faces, Poisoned Places

There is a particular kind of cruelty in being destroyed by something that is smiling at you. Coca-Cola's polar bear — white, fluffy, cheerful — has been selling soda since 1993. Meanwhile, real polar bears are losing their habitat to a warming planet that companies like Coca-Cola have done enormous amounts to accelerate. That is not irony. That is contempt.

Coca-Cola and PepsiCo are not just environmental offenders in the abstract — they are on-the-ground water extractors. In Chiapas, one of Mexico's poorest and most water-stressed states, Coca-Cola's bottling operations have pulled millions of liters from local aquifers that indigenous communities depend on for basic survival. Entire villages have had their wells run dry while nearby facilities fill plastic bottles by the truckload. PepsiCo has faced similar accusations across Latin America and South Asia, where local communities are left to fight over whatever remains after industrial extraction drains the land.

Then there is OpenAI — the company that claims to be building artificial intelligence "for the benefit of all humanity." A noble motto, until you ask: which humanity? OpenAI's data centers consume billions of liters of water annually for cooling, often placed in regions already experiencing drought and scarcity. The tech industry has mastered the language of progress while practicing the oldest logic of extraction: take what you need, leave the bill for someone else.

Chevron earns a category of its own. In Ecuador's Amazon region, decades of drilling left behind open waste pits and oil-contaminated water that indigenous communities drank, bathed in, and fished from. Cancer rates, miscarriages, and childhood illness soared. When courts ordered Chevron to pay billions in damages, the company launched a years-long legal war against the communities and lawyers who had held it accountable — rather than clean anything up. Chevron still runs advertisements about "the human energy" powering the world. The humans living downstream of their abandoned wells would describe it differently.

What unites these companies is not just the scale of harm, but the sophistication of their deflection. Sustainability reports. Net-zero pledges. Conservation sponsorships. They are very good at performing responsibility while continuing, largely uninterrupted, with the practices that caused the damage in the first place. The mascots and the glossy reports are not evidence of reform — they are evidence of how well these companies understand the value of a good image.
