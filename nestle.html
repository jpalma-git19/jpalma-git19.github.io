Nestlé: When a Corporation Targets Mothers and Calls It Marketing

Nestlé represents something even more deliberate than environmental destruction: a company that targeted newborn babies and engineered their dependence on a product their families could not afford, in conditions where using that product could kill them.

Beginning in the 1970s, Nestlé flooded developing countries across Africa, Asia, and Latin America with infant formula. Their representatives, dressed in white uniforms resembling nurses, visited hospitals and maternity wards handing out free samples to new mothers. The samples were not an act of generosity — they were a calculated biological trap. When a mother stops breastfeeding, even briefly, her milk supply diminishes and eventually disappears. By the time the free sample ran out, the mother could no longer breastfeed and was now entirely dependent on purchasing formula to keep her child alive.

In communities where poverty was the norm and clean water was scarce, this dependency became lethal. Families who could not afford enough formula diluted it with contaminated water. Malnourished infants, stripped of the immune protection breastmilk provides, died in numbers that horrified the global health community. The WHO and UNICEF estimated that the collapse of breastfeeding in developing countries — driven significantly by aggressive formula marketing — contributed to over one million infant deaths per year.

None of this was unknown to Nestlé's leadership. There was no informed consent given to mothers who received samples. No disclosure that accepting free formula might permanently end their ability to breastfeed. No honest accounting of the risks of mixing formula without reliable clean water. There was only the imperative to create a market and capture it — consequences be damned. The international backlash produced the WHO International Code of Marketing of Breast-milk Substitutes in 1981, passed 118 nations to one. Nestlé became the subject of one of the longest consumer boycotts in history.

The company has repeatedly pledged to comply with WHO guidelines and has repeatedly been found in violation of them. Nestlé today brands itself as a nutrition and wellness company — warm imagery of families, children, and nature. It is worth remembering that this is the same company that sent agents disguised as nurses into maternity wards to create a dependency that killed babies. The rebranding does not erase the history. It simply bets that we won't remember it.
